# How a resume is screened, and what that implies

Three different readers look at a resume, in this order, and they want different things.

**1. The applicant tracking system (ATS).** Most employers run one. It parses your document into
fields and lets a recruiter search and filter. What it rewards: standard section headings, plain
formatting, and the JD's own vocabulary where it is true of you. What it punishes: tables and
columns it cannot parse, headers in images, and keyword stuffing — which a recruiter sees the
moment they open the file. The ATS does not "score" you against the job in the way the folklore
says; it makes you findable or invisible.

**2. The recruiter, six seconds at a time.** A recruiter is screening for the hiring manager's
must-haves, fast: title, years, the two or three named skills, location and authorization. The
first third of the page decides whether the rest is read. This is why the tailored resume in this
workbench reorders and re-emphasises for one JD — not to say more, but to put the matching facts
where six seconds will find them.

**3. The hiring manager, who will ask about every line.** This reader assumes everything on the
page is a claim you are prepared to defend in detail. A bullet you cannot talk through for two
minutes is a liability, not an asset. This is the reader the fact tags exist for: a `VERIFIED` fact
goes on the page in full; an `EXPOSURE` fact goes on only if you have decided, knowingly, that you
can hold a conversation about what you did and did not do.

## What follows

- Write facts, not phrases. "Built the PXE provisioning platform for 200 machines a day" is a fact;
  "results-driven infrastructure professional" is not a claim anyone can check.
- Keep the real numbers as written. A rounded-up number is the first thing a hiring manager probes.
- Use the JD's words only where the fact supports them. Matching the vocabulary of a requirement
  you do not meet is the specific way a resume becomes a bad interview.
- One resume per direction, one patch per JD. The facts never change; the order and the emphasis do.
