# Practice

Written preparation has a ceiling: a card you can read is not an answer you can say. The last step
is out loud, timed, and critiqued.

## A practice run

1. Take one question card. Read the question — or have it read to you — and answer **out loud**,
   as you would in the room. Record it on your phone.
2. Do not stop at ninety seconds, but notice it. Real interviews do not cut you off; running long
   is a thing to fix, not a rule to obey.
3. Listen back with the card open and mark, point by point:
   - which of the card's expected points you covered, and which you missed;
   - how long it ran, and where the filler words clustered;
   - one sentence you would say differently.
4. If the answer worked — actually worked, on a real person — paste it into the card verbatim,
   imperfections included, under `## Spoken`. That is the only kind of answer the bank keeps.

## Why not a score

A score is a number the grader invented, and it tells you nothing you can change before the next
run. "You missed the point about the stopping condition, and you ran two minutes on a one-minute
question" tells you exactly what to do. The critique is the product; a number would be decoration.

## Delivery, not vocabulary

The words people stop on out loud are rarely the technical ones. Measured on the author: the stalls
were *straightforward, collaboration, authority, evaluated* — ordinary English with consonant
clusters and moveable stress. Keep a pronunciation glossary (`facts/lexicon.md`) of the words *you*
stop on, and annotate a talk track's hard words with a respelling you can read at speed. A talk
track that reads well silently and collapses in the room has failed in the specific way that
matters.

## The one line

**Prepared, spoken, critiqued, repeated.** A new written draft never counts as practice, however
good it is.
